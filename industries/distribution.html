Why distribution margins depend on the system

Distribution runs on thin margins and high transaction volume, which makes it the business type where small systematic errors cost the most. A price applied wrongly on one invoice is trivial; the same rule applied wrongly for six months is a serious number.

Putting pricing, schemes and credit rules into the system takes them out of individual judgement. The invoice is right because the rule is right, and when a rule needs to change it changes in one place.

The same applies to stock. Batch and expiry handling with first-expiry-first-out picking turns expiry from an annual write-off into a weekly report that someone can still act on, which for food, pharma and chemicals usually pays for the implementation on its own.

FAQ

Questions we are asked before signing

Can it handle customer-specific pricing and schemes?

Yes. Price lists, pricing rules, quantity slabs, free-goods schemes and promotional periods are all configurable, and they apply automatically at order entry rather than being remembered by the person keying it.

Does it support van sales or route distribution?

Yes. Each van is modelled as a warehouse, stock is loaded against it, and sales and returns are recorded on the route. Combined with a driver app this closes the loop between what left the godown and what was collected.

How does credit control work in practice?

Limits and ageing rules are checked when the order is entered, with an override that requires authority and leaves an audit trail. The point is not to block business but to make the decision visible.

Can we see profitability by item and by customer?

Yes, from live transactions including landed cost. For distributors this is often the first time the genuinely unprofitable lines and accounts become visible.
